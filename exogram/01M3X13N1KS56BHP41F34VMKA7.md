## Inputs

A pitch has one subject, one reader, one ask and a length. If one is missing, stop and ask the lead, since a pitch to a guessed reader persuades nobody.

- The subject is the thing pitched.
- A reader is a person or a role a message can be addressed to.

## Rules

### 1. Message

The reader keeps one point.

- The first two sentences say what the subject is, in words the reader already uses, and what it makes possible. That is the message.
- Each paragraph opens with a sentence that supports the message.
- The last sentence is the ask.

### 2. Whole before part

A pitch is no specification.

- The bigger picture is the subject as a whole and what people can do with it.
- A single part or feature is an example, never the topic of the pitch.
- Each paragraph names one thing a person can do and why this reader cares.
- At most one sentence per paragraph says how a part works.

### 3. Own argument

A reader who knows the answered text stops at the first bent quote.

- The pitch runs on its own argument.
- When it answers a text, it takes up at most three ideas of that text, in the order its own message needs.
- It takes no heading from that text.
- Each idea gets at most one quote.
- Quote and paraphrase keep the meaning and the hedge of the authors.

### 4. Scene

A reader leaves at the first invented case.

- One scene shows the message: a person, a piece of work, what the person gains.
- It follows the first two sentences and takes at most four sentences.
- The scene is an event a source records, or it is marked as what the design does.

### 5. State in the verb

An expert checks, and one plan told as fact discredits the rest. This rule sets verb and sentence subject and never shrinks the claim.

- What the reader can use today is told in present tense. "We", "here" and "today" belong to those sentences only.
- A designed part is named as a design once, at first mention. After that the pitch says in plain present tense what the design does, with the design as the subject of the sentence.
- Only the building is told in future tense.
- A number, a scale or an "every" appears only where a source records it.
- No sentence says the opposite of a limit the subject's own description admits.

### 6. Limits

A list of limits buries the message.

- Limits get no paragraph of their own.
- A limit is named in one clause, and only where the reader would meet it by doing the ask.

### 7. Terms

The reader has never met the terms of the subject.

- A term of the subject is a word used in a sense the reader's dictionary lacks.
- At most three terms of the subject appear besides its name.
- Each is explained in plain words at first use.

### 8. Specific sentences

A sentence that fits any pitch tells the reader nothing.

- Every sentence says something true only of the subject or of this reader.
- A paragraph ends on a fact or on the next step.

## Check

- Before finishing, quote to the lead the words in the draft that meet each rule.
- Where no words meet a rule, rewrite.
- The check goes in the reply, never in the pitch.
